## Knowledge absorbed from pages 601-800

### 1. Swine influenza is an acute respiratory disease with narrow diagnostic timing

IAV-S mainly replicates in respiratory epithelium and is best detected during the febrile acute phase. Useful specimens include nasal or pharyngeal swabs, trachea, lung, respiratory lymphoid tissues, bronchoalveolar lavage, and oral fluids for herd-level screening. The diagnostic answer should name the timing problem: after the short shedding window, PCR positivity may fall and serology or paired samples become more relevant.

Operational rules:

- no clinical sign or lesion is pathognomonic for IAV-S
- acute high fever, anorexia, inactivity, tachypnea, dyspnea, sudden herd onset, high morbidity, low mortality, and recovery within days support but do not prove influenza
- uncomplicated IAV-S gross lesions are viral pneumonia, commonly cranioventral consolidation with a clear margin; bacterial coinfections may obscure the pattern
- RT-PCR is useful for rapid screening, but weak positives can represent degraded rather than infectious virus
- antigen rapid tests are convenient but may lack sensitivity and do not subtype the virus
- oral fluids are useful for population screening, while isolation and sequencing are usually less successful than from individual respiratory specimens
- vaccine strain match matters because HA-directed immunity is strain/subtype specific
- inactivated vaccines reduce lung viral load and clinical disease better than they block upper-airway shedding or transmission
- vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease is a warning condition when heterologous strains in the same HA subtype are sufficiently drifted

Dataset impact: influenza answers should not diagnose from fever/cough alone, should not overtrust late nasal swabs, and should distinguish individual diagnostic samples from herd screening samples.

### 2. Paramyxoviruses add rare but important reproductive, neurologic, and zoonotic differentials

The paramyxovirus chapter is useful because it prevents the wiki from treating rare viral agents as generic neurologic disease.

Key anchors:

- porcine rubulavirus or blue eye disease combines piglet neurologic disease, corneal opacity, reproductive loss, and boar orchitis/epididymitis; brain is the preferred tissue for virus detection, and paired serology may support diagnosis
- Menangle virus is a rare reproductive syndrome characterized by reduced liveborn piglets, mummified fetuses of varying sizes, stillbirths, congenital defects, and short-duration infection; it should be compared with PPV, EMCV, pestiviruses, PRV, JEV, PRRSV, and rubulavirus
- Nipah virus in pigs is a severe public-health boundary disease; pig disease may include respiratory and neurologic signs, but the major dataset risk is failure to block casual farm-management advice in suspected cases
- porcine parainfluenza virus 1 is a respiratory virus with incomplete disease-causality certainty, so positive PCR should be interpreted with syndrome, coinfections, and pathology

Dataset impact: rare paramyxoviruses should be retrieved for differential diagnosis and public-health boundaries, not used as routine first-line explanations unless the epidemiology fits.

### 3. Porcine parvovirus reproductive failure depends on fetal age and immune status

The parvovirus chapter strengthens reproductive-failure logic. PPV is primarily a reproductive pathogen of susceptible gilts/sows. Adult illness is not the center of the syndrome. The outcome depends on fetal age at infection and whether the fetus is immunocompetent.

Operational rules:

- early fetal infection may cause embryonic death, return to estrus, small litters, mummification, stillbirths, or SMEDI-like patterns
- mummified fetuses of different sizes in the same litter support infection spreading among fetuses over time
- abortion is not the classic dominant PPV presentation compared with fetal death and mummification
- diagnosis should combine reproductive pattern, fetal material, virus or antigen/nucleic-acid detection, and serology with awareness that widespread exposure/vaccination can complicate interpretation
- vaccination and herd immunity are central control tools; naive gilts are the critical risk group

Dataset impact: PPV should be strongly considered for mummification and small litters without prominent sow illness, but not used to explain every abortion or weak piglet syndrome.

### 4. Pestiviruses require high-risk differentiation among CSF, APPV, Bungowannah, and ruminant pestiviruses

The pestivirus chapter is critical for regulatory and differential diagnosis.

Classical swine fever:

- CSF is a high-consequence disease with variable virulence, so absence of classic severe signs does not exclude it
- clinical findings may include fever, depression, anorexia, conjunctivitis, neurologic signs, cyanosis, diarrhea or constipation, leukopenia, reproductive failure, and high mortality in severe outbreaks
- lesions can include lymphoid hemorrhage, splenic infarcts, renal petechiae, button ulcers, and lymphoid depletion, but gross lesions are not sufficient for diagnosis
- diagnosis must rely on laboratory detection and official confirmation, especially because CSF overlaps with ASF, septicemic salmonellosis, erysipelas, PRRSV, PDNS, and other septicemic diseases
- wild boar and movement of infected pigs/products are important epidemiologic considerations

Other pestiviruses:

- APPV is strongly associated with congenital tremor type A-II and should be considered in neonatal shaking piglets rather than adult neurologic syndromes
- Bungowannah virus is linked to reproductive loss and myocarditis-like outcomes in its known context, but it is geographically and epidemiologically restricted
- BVDV and border disease viruses can infect pigs but are usually spillover/cross-species differential considerations rather than routine swine pathogens

Dataset impact: CSF prompts must trigger regulatory caution; APPV answers should focus on congenital tremor and avoid broad overdiagnosis.

### 5. Picornaviruses create the core vesicular-disease and neurologic differential block

The picornavirus chapter is one of the most important additions because vesicular lesions are a major safety boundary.

FMD:

- FMD in pigs is a high-consequence vesicular disease and cannot be distinguished reliably from other vesicular diseases by gross appearance alone
- pigs can generate large amounts of aerosolized virus and can amplify spread even when other species are involved
- clinical signs include acute fever, lameness, reluctance to move, vesicles or erosions on snout, tongue, coronary bands, interdigital skin, teats, or oral mucosa
- laboratory confirmation and official response are mandatory in suspected cases
- vaccination/control discussion must stay within official program boundaries and not become farm-level ad hoc advice

Swine vesicular disease and Senecavirus A:

- SVD and Senecavirus A can produce vesicular lesions clinically indistinguishable from FMD, vesicular stomatitis, and vesicular exanthema
- Senecavirus A may also be associated with neonatal morbidity/mortality in some outbreaks, but vesicular differential exclusion remains the first priority
- laboratory testing is mandatory for vesicular lesions because management consequences differ sharply among agents

Other picornaviruses:

- EMCV can cause sudden death, myocarditis, reproductive problems, and neonatal losses, often with rodent-related epidemiologic clues
- porcine teschovirus can be associated with polioencephalomyelitis, reproductive disorders, diarrhea, pneumonia, pericarditis, myocarditis, and occasional skin-lesion associations, but detection must be tied to lesion distribution and exclusion of alternatives
- porcine sapelovirus and kobuvirus detections in feces require caution because presence does not always prove causality

Dataset impact: any vesicle on pig feet/snout/oral tissues must trigger FMD/SVD/SVA/VS/VES differential and a regulatory-lab boundary before treatment suggestions.

### 6. PRRSV is a persistent, diverse, syndrome-level disease that resists single-test simplification

The PRRSV chapter gives the wiki strong disease-specific content.

Operational rules:

- PRRSV causes reproductive failure in breeding herds and respiratory/growth disease in growing pigs, but expression varies with strain, immunity, coinfections, herd flow, and management
- PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 are highly diverse; ORF5/RFLP descriptions are not reliable proxies for virulence, protection, or full genetic relatedness
- shedding can occur in multiple routes, persistence can occur after apparent recovery, and vertical transmission is important in reproductive disease
- diagnostic specimens may include serum, oral fluids, processing fluids, tissues, bronchoalveolar lavage, fetal tissues, and reproductive materials depending on the question
- RT-PCR detects nucleic acid and must be interpreted with clinical stage, sample type, and herd history; sequencing helps monitor strain relationships but does not alone prove source or virulence
- serology supports exposure and herd monitoring but is not a simple indicator of protection
- modified-live vaccination can reduce clinical impact but has cross-protection and reversion/recombination/strain-mismatch concerns; it does not guarantee eradication
- acclimatization, herd closure, load-close-homogenize, partial depopulation, test-and-removal, biosecurity, needle hygiene, and surveillance must be chosen according to herd status and goals

Dataset impact: PRRSV answers must be herd-program answers, not just single-pig treatment answers. They should also avoid claiming that a PCR-positive result alone proves causality in a complex respiratory or reproductive outbreak.

### 7. Swinepox is a skin disease with vector and differential boundaries

Swinepox supports integumentary differential diagnosis:

- lesions progress through papules, vesicles/pustules, crusts, and scabs, commonly on sparsely haired skin
- disease is usually self-limiting, but severity varies with age, hygiene, lice/vector burden, and coinfections
- diagnosis can use clinical pattern, histopathology, intracytoplasmic inclusions, electron microscopy, immunostaining, PCR, or virus detection when needed
- differential diagnosis should include exudative epidermitis, mange, insect bites, vesicular diseases, pox-like lesions, and other papular/crusting dermatoses

Dataset impact: swinepox should not be treated as a reportable vesicular disease by default, but vesicular/crusting lesions near feet/oral tissues still require careful differential triage.

### 8. Rotaviruses strengthen neonatal and post-weaning diarrhea differentiation

The rotavirus chapter adds concrete enteric-disease content:

- rotaviruses infect enterocytes and cause malabsorptive diarrhea through villous injury, with disease severity shaped by age, immunity, viral group, coinfections, and environmental pressure
- rotavirus A, B, and C differ in epidemiology and diagnostic availability; mixed infections and other enteric pathogens are common
- lesions may include thin-walled fluid-filled intestines and microscopic villous atrophy, but gross lesions are not specific
- diagnosis should use feces or intestinal contents/tissues from acutely affected untreated pigs, ideally with age-matched controls and testing for other enteric pathogens
- maternal lactogenic immunity and hygiene are key control concepts

Dataset impact: rotavirus answers should be part of a neonatal/post-weaning diarrhea differential with ETEC, C. perfringens, coccidia, PED/TGE/PDCoV, salmonellosis, and management/environmental factors.

### 9. Retroviruses, rhabdoviruses, and togaviruses are primarily boundary and differential topics

Porcine endogenous retroviruses are most relevant to xenotransplantation and biosafety risk assessment, not routine herd disease diagnosis. Rabies in pigs is rare but has major public-health implications and requires neurologic differential caution. Vesicular stomatitis is another vesicular look-alike that cannot be separated from FMD/SVD/SVA by appearance alone. Togaviruses such as Getah, Sagiyama, Ross River, and eastern equine encephalitis are mostly geographically and epidemiologically constrained differential/public-health anchors.

Dataset impact: these topics should be retrieved to prevent unsafe reassurance or wrong regulatory triage, not to inflate routine farm-level probabilities.

### 10. Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae requires early recognition, lesion pattern, carrier detection, and stewardship

The Actinobacillosis chapter is the first major bacterial content in this segment.

Operational rules:

- App causes contagious pleuropneumonia with acute fever, anorexia, dyspnea, cyanosis, sudden death, and severe fibrinohemorrhagic/necrotizing lung lesions
- gross lesions often involve sharply demarcated dark consolidated lung areas with fibrinous pleuritis, especially in caudodorsal lung, but chronic lesions and sequestra can occur
- diagnosis should combine clinical outbreak pattern, necropsy lesions, culture/PCR/typing, and awareness of carrier pigs
- tonsillar carriers can maintain infection and complicate eradication; absence of clinical signs does not prove herd freedom
- antimicrobial treatment is most effective very early and does not eliminate herd infection or carrier status
- antimicrobial choice must consider susceptibility, local legality, label, withdrawal time, and stewardship; textbook drug examples cannot be converted into direct China dosing advice
- vaccines can reduce clinical signs and lesions but may be serotype-specific and usually do not eliminate carriers

Dataset impact: App answers should identify acute mortality and pleuropneumonia urgency while avoiding unverified dose/withdrawal recommendations.

### 11. Actinobacillus suis and other Actinobacillus species broaden septicemia and respiratory differentials

Actinobacillus suis can cause septicemia, sudden death, arthritis, pneumonia, pleuritis, endocarditis, meningitis, and reproductive problems in susceptible or high-health herds. It can resemble App, Streptococcus suis, Glasser's disease, salmonellosis, erysipelas, and other septicemic conditions. Culture and lesion distribution are important.

Dataset impact: when sudden death or septicemia is present, the answer should include necropsy, culture, histopathology, and herd-source risk rather than assuming App or S. suis from respiratory signs alone.

### 12. Bordetella bronchiseptica is a colonizer, primary pathogen, and coinfection amplifier

The Bordetellosis chapter helps correct over-simple respiratory answers:

- B. bronchiseptica spreads readily by direct contact and aerosol, causes high morbidity and usually low mortality unless very young pigs or coinfections are involved
- uncomplicated disease includes sneezing, nasal and ocular discharge, dry cough, tracheobronchitis, and nonprogressive turbinate atrophy
- severe neonatal cases may develop necrohemorrhagic bronchopneumonia
- progressive atrophic rhinitis usually requires toxigenic Pasteurella multocida, so B. bronchiseptica alone should not be blamed for severe snout distortion without considering P. multocida
- diagnosis uses nasal swabs, postmortem lung washings, biopsy material, culture with suitable media, PCR, and turbinate scoring where rhinitis is the question
- vaccination and antimicrobials can reduce disease expression, but bacterins usually do not prevent colonization and vaccinated pigs may still carry and transmit
- drug selection must be stewardship- and label-bound; ceftiofur should not be assumed optimal for Bordetella-containing mixed infections

Dataset impact: Bordetella answers should separate colonization, primary respiratory disease, NPAR, PAR coinfection, and PRDC amplification.

## Generation checklist additions

Generated answers should fail review if they:

- diagnose IAV-S, CSF, FMD, PRRSV, App, or Bordetella from signs alone without appropriate diagnostic confirmation
- discuss vesicular lesions without FMD/SVD/SVA/VS/VES differential and official testing boundary
- provide casual treatment or movement advice for suspected FMD or CSF
- use PRRSV ORF5/RFLP labels as a direct virulence or protection guarantee
- equate PCR positivity with causality for rotavirus, PRRSV, kobuvirus, sapelovirus, Bordetella, or other commonly detected agents without clinical-pathologic fit
- recommend antimicrobial doses, routes, durations, or withdrawal times from textbook discussion rather than China label/authority sources
- treat App antimicrobial therapy as herd elimination or carrier clearance
- blame severe progressive atrophic rhinitis on Bordetella alone without toxigenic Pasteurella multocida differential

## Evaluation checklist additions

Evaluators should check whether answers:

- choose sample type according to disease stage and syndrome
- distinguish individual diagnosis from herd surveillance
- distinguish detection, exposure, causality, immunity, and protection
- identify official regulatory boundaries for CSF, FMD, vesicular disease, rabies/Nipah public-health concerns, and high-consequence outbreaks
- name key differential diagnoses rather than giving single-agent conclusions
- state what remains uncertain and what additional samples or official tests are needed
