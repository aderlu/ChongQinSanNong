## Knowledge absorbed from pages 801-1000

### 1. Swine brucellosis is a reproductive, venereal, wildlife, and zoonotic boundary disease

Brucella suis infection in swine is often clinically silent, especially outside reproductive animals, but it can cause infertility, irregular returns, fetal loss, stillbirth, orchitis, epididymitis, lameness, and chronic genital lesions. Abortion can occur, but field presentations often center on fertility loss rather than dramatic abortion storms.

Operational rules:

- B. suis spreads by venereal, oral, contact, semen, aborted material, and wildlife-reservoir routes
- infected boars can lower conception rate and litter size through testicular or epididymal damage
- culture is the definitive diagnostic method, but slow growth, biosafety, chronic-infection recovery limits, and contamination complicate use
- preferred culture samples include vaginal secretions, milk, semen, fetal membranes, aborted fetal stomach contents/spleen/lung, spleen, lymph nodes, mammary gland, uterus, testes, epididymis, and accessory sex glands
- swine serology is better for herd-level screening than individual diagnosis; positive herds need culture or molecular confirmation
- false-positive serology can occur because of cross-reactive organisms such as Yersinia enterocolitica O:9
- B. suis is a public-health risk for farmworkers, veterinarians, abattoir workers, hunters, and people handling infected tissues
- biosecurity, testing of semen/seedstock, and wildlife exclusion are central because available vaccines do not reliably solve the problem

Dataset impact: brucellosis answers must handle reproductive failure and public health together, and must not overtrust individual serology.

### 2. Clostridial diseases split into enteric, histotoxic, and neurotoxic syndromes

The clostridial chapter prevents the wiki from treating clostridia as one generic diarrhea cause.

Core rules:

- C. perfringens type C causes severe neonatal necrohemorrhagic enteritis, often with peracute hemorrhagic diarrhea and rapid death; diagnosis uses compatible lesions, abundant large gram-positive rods, and beta-toxin detection, but toxin degradation can create false negatives
- C. perfringens type A is harder to assign as causal because it can be present in healthy pigs; diagnosis needs compatible lesions and toxin/pathotype interpretation
- C. difficile-associated disease in piglets may involve mesocolonic edema and typhlocolitis; toxin detection and lesion fit matter
- gas gangrene and blackleg are wound/muscle-associated histotoxic syndromes, not routine enteric disease
- tetanus and botulism are neurotoxic syndromes; clinical syndrome and exposure history dominate, while culture alone may not prove causality

Dataset impact: neonatal bloody diarrhea, sudden death, neuromuscular signs, and wound-associated swelling must route to different clostridial logic.

### 3. Colibacillosis must be separated by pathotype, age, syndrome, and virulence factors

The colibacillosis chapter is central for diarrhea and edema disease.

Operational rules:

- neonatal ETEC diarrhea depends on fimbrial adhesion, enterotoxins, age susceptibility, colostral/lactogenic immunity, and environmental exposure
- post-weaning ETEC diarrhea is tied to weaning stress, diet transition, colonization, fimbriae, toxins, and host susceptibility
- edema disease is an E. coli/Shiga-toxin syndrome with vascular injury and neurologic/sudden-death presentations; it is not just severe diarrhea
- EPEC, STEC/EDEC, ExPEC, fatal shock E. coli, coliform mastitis, and nonspecific urinary E. coli disease are distinct syndromes
- diagnosis should not stop at nonspecific E. coli culture because E. coli is common; virulence typing, lesion fit, age, site, and syndrome are needed
- prevention includes colostrum/lactogenic immunity, sow vaccination where appropriate, hygiene, temperature, creep area management, weaning stress reduction, diet and water management, and susceptibility-aware herd strategy
- antimicrobial prophylaxis and treatment discussion must be constrained by susceptibility, stewardship, local regulations, labels, and withdrawal times

Dataset impact: E. coli answers should identify whether the case is neonatal diarrhea, post-weaning diarrhea, edema disease, septicemia, mastitis, or UTI before giving advice.

### 4. Erysipelas spans acute septicemia, skin lesions, arthritis, and endocarditis

Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae can present as acute septicemia/sudden death, fever and diamond-skin lesions, chronic arthritis, or valvular endocarditis.

Operational rules:

- diamond skin lesions support but do not alone prove erysipelas
- acute cases can die before classic skin lesions appear
- chronic arthritis and endocarditis may follow earlier infection or occur without a clear acute history
- differentials include CSF, ASF, salmonellosis, septicemia, urticaria, sunburn/skin trauma, and other causes of arthritis/endocarditis
- diagnosis may use culture, PCR, histopathology, lesion pattern, and response history, but antimicrobial treatment before sampling can reduce recovery
- erysipeloid is a human occupational risk

Dataset impact: erysipelas answers should not rely solely on diamond lesions, and should keep septicemic and chronic forms in view.

### 5. Glasser's disease is a polyserositis/polyarthritis/meningitis syndrome with colonization and coinfection complexity

Haemophilus parasuis / Glaesserella parasuis is part of the upper respiratory ecology in many herds, but virulent strains can cause systemic disease.

Operational rules:

- clinical disease often follows loss of maternal immunity, stress, coinfection, movement, or mixing
- lesions include fibrinous polyserositis, polyarthritis, meningitis, pleuritis, pericarditis, peritonitis, and sometimes pneumonia
- diagnosis is strongest when organism detection occurs in systemic lesions or normally sterile sites; upper respiratory detection alone is weak evidence
- serovar or genotyping does not reliably predict protection or virulence across herds
- cross-protection among strains is variable, which limits simple vaccine assumptions
- differentials include S. suis, Mycoplasma hyorhinis, App, A. suis, E. coli septicemia, and other causes of polyserositis or meningitis

Dataset impact: Glasser answers should separate colonization from systemic disease and include sterile-site sampling logic.

### 6. Leptospirosis is reproductive, renal, urinary, and zoonotic

Leptospiral infection in pigs varies by serovar and maintenance/adaptation status. It is important for abortions, stillbirths, weak piglets, infertility, renal carriage, urinary shedding, and occupational exposure.

Operational rules:

- clinical patterns include acute illness, reproductive failure, chronic renal infection, urinary shedding, and herd-level infertility
- Pomona, Bratislava/Australis, Tarassovi, Canicola, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Grippotyphosa, and Hardjo contexts differ in maintenance and transmission ecology
- serology is useful but has timing and cross-reaction limits; MAT interpretation requires paired or herd-level context
- direct detection may use fetal tissues, placenta, kidney, urine, or reproductive material depending on stage
- culture is slow and difficult; PCR or other direct methods may be more practical in some diagnostic questions
- artificial insemination and semen control matter when reproductive transmission is suspected
- vaccination can reduce disease but is serovar/context dependent and does not replace biosecurity and reservoir control

Dataset impact: leptospirosis answers should tie reproductive signs to urine/kidney/semen/reservoir ecology and public-health precautions.

### 7. Mycoplasmosis includes respiratory, serosal, joint, and hemotropic syndromes

The mycoplasmosis chapter supports several syndrome pages.

Operational rules:

- M. hyopneumoniae is a major enzootic pneumonia and PRDC contributor; it impairs mucociliary clearance and creates chronic cough, poor growth, and cranioventral lung consolidation
- diagnosis should combine herd signs, lung lesions, histopathology, PCR/serology, and population timing; single-agent detection may not explain PRDC alone
- control can include herd flow, biosecurity, vaccination, acclimatization, medication where legal, and eradication programs; vaccination reduces disease but does not necessarily prevent colonization or eliminate infection
- M. hyorhinis is associated with polyserositis and arthritis, especially in younger pigs, and overlaps with Glasser's disease and S. suis
- M. hyosynoviae is more associated with arthritis/lameness in older growing pigs
- M. suis causes hemotropic disease with anemia, icterus, reproductive effects, and stress-associated clinical expression; PCR is more reliable than smear alone when available

Dataset impact: mycoplasma answers should identify the species and syndrome instead of saying "mycoplasma infection" generically.

### 8. Pasteurellosis requires separating progressive atrophic rhinitis, pneumonia, and septicemia

Pasteurella multocida is not one disease in pigs.

Operational rules:

- toxigenic P. multocida is central to progressive atrophic rhinitis and should be considered with Bordetella bronchiseptica and turbinate/snoutu changes
- non-toxigenic or opportunistic P. multocida often acts as a secondary respiratory pathogen in pneumonia/PRDC
- septicemic pasteurellosis is a distinct and less common syndrome requiring septicemia differential diagnosis
- diagnosis depends on lesion distribution, culture/PCR/toxin testing, and the question being asked: PAR, pneumonia, or septicemia
- vaccination/control differs by syndrome; PAR toxoid/bacterin logic is not the same as pneumonia control

Dataset impact: answers must not treat any P. multocida isolation from lung or nose as proof of primary disease.

### 9. Proliferative enteropathy is an ileitis complex with chronic and hemorrhagic forms

Lawsonia intracellularis causes proliferative enteropathy with several forms.

Operational rules:

- grower-finisher pigs may show chronic diarrhea, poor growth, and thickened ileum
- acute proliferative hemorrhagic enteropathy can cause pallor, melena, sudden death, and severe intestinal hemorrhage, often in older growers/replacement animals
- lesions involve intestinal mucosal proliferation, especially ileum, with thickened mucosa and sometimes necrosis or hemorrhage
- diagnosis should combine lesion site, histopathology, intracellular curved bacteria, immunohistochemistry, PCR, serology, and herd pattern
- PCR on feces can support diagnosis but should be interpreted with clinical stage and lesion evidence
- treatment and vaccination discussion must remain label- and jurisdiction-bound

Dataset impact: ileitis answers should separate chronic poor-doer diarrhea from acute hemorrhagic sudden death presentations.

### 10. Salmonellosis is both pig disease and pork/public-health risk

Salmonella chapters provide enterocolitis, septicemia, carrier, and food-safety anchors.

Operational rules:

- S. Typhimurium and monophasic variants are commonly linked to enterocolitis and pork contamination concerns
- S. Choleraesuis is more associated with septicemia, pneumonia, hepatitis, and systemic disease
- shedding and carrier states complicate interpretation of fecal culture
- diagnosis should combine clinical signs, lesions, culture/PCR, serotyping, and herd/food-chain context
- public-health and antimicrobial-resistance boundaries matter
- prevention/control involves sanitation, feed/water hygiene, rodent and bird control, all-in/all-out, biosecurity, stress reduction, and slaughter-chain awareness

Dataset impact: salmonellosis answers should separate enterocolitis from septicemia and include food-safety context when appropriate.

### 11. Staphylococcosis includes exudative epidermitis, S. aureus lesions, and MRSA public-health boundaries

Staphylococcus hyicus causes exudative epidermitis, especially in young pigs, with greasy exudative skin lesions, dehydration, and possible mortality. Diagnosis should consider lesion pattern, culture, toxin context, and differentials such as mange, swinepox, dermatophytosis, trauma, and other bacterial dermatitis.

Staphylococcus aureus can be involved in abscesses, arthritis, mastitis, septicemia, and wound infections. MRSA is a public-health and occupational boundary rather than a routine reason to give unsourced treatment advice.

Dataset impact: skin disease answers should distinguish exudative epidermitis from swinepox and parasitic/allergic lesions, and should not ignore zoonotic MRSA boundaries.

### 12. Streptococcus suis is a neurologic, septicemic, arthritic, and zoonotic disease with carrier complexity

S. suis is a major cause of meningitis, septicemia, arthritis, polyserositis, endocarditis, pneumonia, and sudden death. It also has a strong public-health boundary for people handling pigs or pork.

Operational rules:

- healthy pigs can carry S. suis in tonsils/upper respiratory tract, so carrier detection does not equal disease
- diagnosis is strongest from normally sterile sites or lesions, such as brain/meninges, joint fluid, blood, spleen, heart valves, or serosal lesions
- culture, PCR, serotyping, and histopathology should be matched to clinical lesions
- antimicrobial treatment decisions require susceptibility and stewardship; prevention should reduce predisposing stressors and coinfections
- vaccines and autogenous products have variable protection because strain diversity and cross-protection are limited
- eradication is difficult and generally not a simple medication problem

Dataset impact: neurologic piglet or nursery-pig answers should include S. suis but must sample sterile sites and address zoonotic precautions.

### 13. Swine dysentery and brachyspiral colitis are large-intestinal diarrheal diseases with detection-causality traps

Brachyspira chapters strengthen grower-finisher diarrhea differentiation.

Operational rules:

- swine dysentery is a mucohemorrhagic large-intestinal disease, classically associated with B. hyodysenteriae and related strongly beta-hemolytic Brachyspira species
- lesions are mainly cecum and colon, not small-intestine villous atrophy
- diagnosis should combine clinical syndrome, large-intestinal lesions, culture/PCR/speciation, and histopathology when needed
- antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance monitoring matter for control decisions
- eradication requires herd-structure planning, cleaning/disinfection, replacement-source control, and often medication or depopulation strategies
- B. pilosicoli-associated colitis is often milder and can occur with fecal shedding in clinically normal pigs, so fecal positivity alone is insufficient

Dataset impact: grower-finisher diarrhea answers should separate swine dysentery, brachyspiral colitis, proliferative enteropathy, salmonellosis, whipworm, and diet/feed issues.

### 14. Tuberculosis opening adds abattoir, lymph-node, and public-health boundaries

The tuberculosis chapter begins near the end of this markdown segment. It shows that swine TB is often subclinical and detected at slaughter through cervical or mesenteric lymph-node lesions. Mycobacterium avium complex and related nontuberculous mycobacteria are important in pigs; public-health interpretation must distinguish exposure risk, immunocompromised-host risks, and the absence of simple evidence that pork exposure is a major human MAC route.

Dataset impact: TB answers should not rely on clinical signs alone and should route suspicious granulomatous lymph-node lesions to official meat inspection, culture/PCR, and public-health context.

## Generation checklist additions

Generated answers should fail review if they:

- diagnose bacterial disease from culture/PCR positivity alone without lesion, site, age, and syndrome context
- treat individual swine brucellosis serology as definitive without herd and culture/molecular confirmation context
- provide antimicrobial dose, route, duration, prophylaxis, or withdrawal instructions from textbook content rather than China label/official sources
- ignore zoonotic/public-health boundaries for Brucella suis, leptospirosis, Salmonella, S. suis, MRSA, or mycobacteria
- call every neonatal diarrhea case ETEC without considering clostridia, rotavirus, coronavirus, coccidia, and management
- call every grower-finisher bloody diarrhea swine dysentery without considering Lawsonia and Salmonella
- assign Pasteurella or Bordetella as primary disease from nasal/lung isolation alone
- diagnose Glasser's disease or S. suis from upper respiratory carriage alone

## Evaluation checklist additions

Evaluators should check whether answers:

- separate pathotype, age group, and syndrome
- choose sterile-site or lesion-site samples when colonization is common
- distinguish exposure, carriage, infection, causality, immunity, and protection
- handle zoonotic/public-health risk without overclaiming
- identify antimicrobial-stewardship and label/withdrawal boundaries
- include differential diagnoses that share the same age, lesion distribution, and clinical pattern
