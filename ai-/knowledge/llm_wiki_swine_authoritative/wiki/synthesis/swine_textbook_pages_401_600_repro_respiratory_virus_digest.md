## Knowledge absorbed from pages 401-600

### 1. Reproductive failure needs herd records, dam signs, fetal stage, and sample strategy

The reproductive-system continuation provides a concrete diagnostic structure for herd reproductive problems:

- delayed puberty or poor gilt response requires review of gilt age, boar contact, nutrition, disease/toxin history, backfat/growth, estrus detection, and real-time ultrasound findings
- prolonged wean-to-estrus interval should separate missed heat from true anestrus and evaluate parity, lactation weight loss, feed intake, heat stress, boar stimulation, and lactational estrus
- conception and farrowing-rate problems should distinguish regular returns, irregular returns, late returns, pseudopregnancy, pregnancy-diagnosis errors, semen handling, insemination timing, ovulation pattern, season, heat, movement stress, and infectious pregnancy loss
- vulvar discharge can be physiologic or pathologic depending on timing, appearance, odor, pregnancy/lactation stage, systemic signs, and herd pattern
- boar reproductive problems should include heat stress, fever, mycotoxins, overuse, semen quality, semen contamination, and infectious agents transmitted through semen

Dataset impact: reproductive failure answers should not jump from abortion to one pathogen. They should first organize the herd record, reproductive timing, sow illness status, fetal stage, semen/AI context, and toxin/management context.

### 2. Abortion diagnosis requires mechanism-specific samples

The abortion sections are especially useful for the current sampling gap.

Core rules:

- abortion may occur well after the initiating cause, so diagnosis is retrospective and depends on pathogenesis
- fetal/placental infection and maternal systemic illness require different sampling strategies
- if aborting sows are clinically ill, collect samples from both the sow and fetuses
- for acute maternal illness, sow samples may be more useful than fetal samples for agents that do not infect fetuses, such as influenza A virus
- if fetal or placental infection is suspected, focus on placenta, multiple fetuses, and dam serology or seroconversion
- sample enough fetuses and litters: the text supports sampling multiple fetuses per litter and multiple litters because not all fetuses are infected at abortion
- preferred fetal specimens include formalin-fixed and fresh/chilled brain, heart, lung, liver, spleen, kidney, placenta, skeletal muscle, fetal thoracic fluid, fetal stomach contents, and dam serum or other dam samples when indicated
- fetal stomach contents are relatively useful for bacterial culture because they are less contaminated
- serology is useful for proving herd exposure, demonstrating seroconversion, or ruling out agents when negative results accumulate
- fetal thoracic fluid antibody results are not generally reliable for agent-specific diagnosis; nonspecific fetal IgG/IgM indicates in utero antigen exposure but not the exact agent
- gross lesions are often absent in viral abortion; microscopic lesions such as nonsuppurative encephalitis, myocarditis, vasculitis, and multifocal necrosis are more helpful

Dataset impact: answers about abortion should name sample types and explain why the dam/fetus/placenta mix depends on whether the initiating event is fetal infection or maternal illness.

### 3. Reproductive differential table improves disease-specific reproductive QA

The reproductive chapter gives a practical disease-differential skeleton:

- PRRSV: late-term abortion, stillbirths, weak-born pigs; fetal thoracic fluid/tissues or acutely ill dam serum can support PCR depending on timing
- PCV2: weak births, stillbirths, fetal mummification, fetal cardiac lesions; fetal heart/lung PCR and fetal heart IHC are important
- PPV: embryonic death, resorption, small litters, fetal mummification; mummified fetal tissues can be tested
- PRV/Aujeszky: embryonic death, mummification, abortion, stillbirths, weak-born pigs; lung, liver, spleen, brain, kidney support detection
- IAV: fever, lethargy, coughing, dyspnea in sows; fetuses are not appropriate for identifying IAV as the initiating cause
- JEV, CSF, ASF, leptospirosis, Brucella suis, atypical pestiviruses, PCMV, rubulavirus, Menangle virus, EMCV, enteroviruses/teschoviruses, carbon monoxide, and zearalenone belong in the reproductive-failure differential space

Dataset impact: the reproductive-failure syndrome page and future differential matrix should preserve sow signs, reproductive manifestation, fetal lesions, and recommended diagnostic specimens.

### 4. Respiratory disease is a multifactorial herd problem

The respiratory chapter directly strengthens PRDC and respiratory syndrome pages.

Core rules:

- respiratory disease often emerges when defenses are impaired or overwhelmed by dust, ammonia, gases, temperature stress, dehydration, crowding, mixing, shipping, nutrition, concurrent infection, and primary pathogens
- the mucociliary apparatus, alveolar macrophages, immunoglobulins, and cell-mediated immunity are central respiratory defenses
- primary respiratory pathogens can damage respiratory defenses, allowing secondary bacterial bronchopneumonia
- PRDC is commonly polymicrobial and involves interactions among viruses, bacteria, environment, management, and host factors
- primary respiratory pathogens include IAV, PRRSV, CSFV, PRV, PCV2, M. hyopneumoniae, and A. pleuropneumoniae; opportunistic or secondary bacteria include streptococci, staphylococci, E. coli, Klebsiella, Trueperella, Bordetella, Glaesserella/Haemophilus parasuis, and Pasteurella
- bronchopneumonia, interstitial pneumonia, embolic pneumonia, pleuritis, polyserositis, and chronic pleural adhesions have different pathogenetic meanings
- control should reduce pathogen load, minimize negative influences on respiratory defenses, and maximize host resistance
- management controls include ventilation, dust/ammonia reduction, stocking density, pig flow, age segregation, all-in/all-out, quarantine/acclimatization, biosecurity, and targeted vaccination based on herd pathogen timing
- respiratory monitoring should use a defined diagnostic objective and can include farm inspection, necropsy, histopathology, lab testing, serology, nasal swabs, oral fluids, and slaughter checks

Dataset impact: respiratory answers should not be only "give antibiotics." Good answers should mention environmental/management contributors, primary-secondary pathogen logic, sampling, and monitoring.

### 5. Urinary system adds renal gross-lesion differential and sow UTI/PDS-adjacent context

The urinary chapter helps acute death, hematuria, renal petechiae, kidney lesions, PDNS, cystitis-pyelonephritis, sow mortality, and toxin differentials.

Core rules:

- renal failure may be prerenal, renal, or postrenal
- urine interpretation should consider specific gravity, pH, proteinuria, sediment, inflammation, stress, age, diet, water intake, and lower urinary-tract disease
- kidney petechiae can be septicemic bacterial disease, acute viral disease such as CSF/ASF/cytomegalovirus, electrocution, intoxication, GN, or other causes; neonatal congestion may mimic petechiae
- glomerulonephritis can be immune-mediated and may follow chronic infectious diseases, systemic cytomegalovirus, CSF, ASF, streptococcal abscesses, or PDNS
- cystitis-pyelonephritis in sows can involve urinary pH changes, cloudy/bloodstained urine, renal failure markers, suppurative pelvic/papillary lesions, ureteritis, cystitis, and management/water/hygiene risk
- urolithiasis, hydronephrosis, kidney worm, vitamin D toxicosis, and chronic salt intoxication belong in the urinary differential space
- the renal gross-lesion table is a useful matrix for differentiating petechiae, edema, pale kidneys, infarcts, interstitial nephritis, embolic nephritis, pyelonephritis, fibrosis, cysts, hydronephrosis, and renal tumors

Dataset impact: suspected ASF/CSF/PDNS/septicemia cases with kidney lesions should not rely on kidney petechiae alone; gross distribution, systemic signs, skin lesions, renal failure evidence, and lab confirmation matter.

### 6. Virus overview improves interpretation of PCR, antigen, culture, serology, sequencing, and virus quantitation

The virus overview chapter supports the existing causality and diagnostic-boundary rules:

- virus detection method should match the diagnostic question, disease stage, tissue tropism, sample quality, and expected shedding
- antigen tests, nucleic-acid tests, culture, electron microscopy, antibody tests, sequencing, and quantitative assays answer different questions
- sequence detection can establish presence and relatedness but does not automatically prove causality
- viral taxonomy and genetic diversity matter for differential diagnosis, vaccine match, surveillance, and tracing
- detection of antibodies indicates exposure or immune response, but not necessarily current disease

Dataset impact: judge and arbiter prompts should penalize answers that treat any PCR positive as definitive disease without clinical/pathologic compatibility.

### 7. Adenoviruses are usually low-causality and need conservative interpretation

The adenovirus chapter is useful for one of the short disease pages.

Core rules:

- porcine adenoviruses are widely distributed and can be found in healthy pigs
- reported associations include enteric, respiratory, reproductive, or other lesions, but causal significance is often uncertain
- diagnosis should not be based on detection alone; compatible lesions and exclusion of more likely agents are needed
- there is no strong basis for generating specific treatment, eradication, or vaccine programs from this chapter

Dataset impact: `DIS-001-adenoviruses.md` and low-frequency-virus generation should treat adenovirus as a differential or co-detection unless evidence is strong.

### 8. ASF chapter strongly improves high-risk disease boundaries

The ASF chapter supplies concrete clinical, lesion, sample, and diagnostic boundaries while still requiring A0/A1 sources for China-specific action.

Core rules:

- ASF is not a human infection, but has major animal-health, trade, social, and food-security impact
- ASF should be suspected in compatible high-fever, hemorrhagic, cyanotic, high-mortality, splenomegaly, hemorrhagic lymph-node, renal petechiae, pulmonary edema, abortion, and sudden-death scenarios
- ASF can resemble CSF, erysipelas, septicemic salmonellosis, and other hemorrhagic diseases
- clinical signs and gross lesions alone cannot diagnose ASF; laboratory testing is essential
- useful samples include lymph nodes, kidney, spleen, lung, blood, and serum; wild-boar bone marrow can be useful
- PCR and hemadsorption test are central virus-detection tools; serology is important for surveillance and recovered/subacute/inapparent infections
- low-virulence ASF strains may lack obvious signs or lesions
- when ASF is suspected, pig movement should be restricted and diagnostic testing performed immediately
- no effective treatment or commercial vaccine should be presented as a routine field solution in train-ready QA
- contaminated pork products, fomites, feed/garbage, wild suids, soft ticks, and carrier/surviving pigs can be relevant in epidemiology

Dataset impact: high-risk ASF answers should trigger hard safety/regulatory boundaries and should not recommend empirical treatment, sale, movement, or concealment.

### 9. Circovirus chapter improves PCV2-SD, PCV2 reproductive disease, and PDNS pages

The circovirus chapter provides disease-specific criteria:

- PCV2-SD is multifactorial; PCV2 is necessary, but cofactors and immune stimulation often shape disease expression
- PCV2-SD commonly affects pigs around 2-4 months with wasting, pallor, respiratory distress, diarrhea, sometimes icterus, and early enlarged lymph nodes
- diagnosis of PCV2-SD requires clinical wasting/growth retardation, characteristic moderate-to-severe lymphoid histopathology, and moderate-to-high PCV2 amount in lesions
- qualitative PCR alone is not suitable for diagnosing PCV2-SD because PCV2 is ubiquitous
- IHC/ISH with histopathology is central for tissue-level diagnosis
- PCV2 reproductive disease at late gestation requires late-term abortion/stillbirths, fetal cardiac lesions, and high PCV2 in myocardial lesions and other fetal tissues
- PCV2 early gestation impact is less certain and should be interpreted cautiously
- PDNS is diagnosed by hemorrhagic/necrotizing skin lesions and/or swollen pale kidneys with cortical petechiae plus systemic necrotizing vasculitis and necrotizing fibrinous glomerulonephritis
- PDNS gross lesions may resemble CSF and ASF, so reportable-disease differentials matter

Dataset impact: PCV2 pages and syndrome pages need hard rules against qPCR-only diagnosis and should preserve PCV2-SD, PCV2-RD, and PDNS as distinct clinical entities.

### 10. Coronavirus chapter improves TGE/PRCV/PED/PDCoV differential and lactogenic immunity

The coronavirus chapter is a rich source for enteric coronavirus QA:

- TGE is highly contagious, with vomiting, severe watery diarrhea, dehydration, and high mortality in piglets under 2 weeks in susceptible herds
- epidemic TGE differs from endemic TGE by herd immunity and continuous introduction of susceptible pigs
- endemic TGE in suckling or recently weaned pigs can resemble PEDV, PDCoV, rotavirus, E. coli, and coccidiosis
- PRCV is a respiratory deletion mutant of TGEV, usually subclinical, with contact/airborne spread and little/no fecal shedding
- PRCV antibodies complicate TGEV serology and may provide partial cross-protection
- TGEV causes massive necrosis of jejunal enterocytes and villous atrophy, producing malabsorptive diarrhea, dehydration, acidosis, and protein loss
- PEDV and PDCoV also cause villous atrophy and need differential testing
- passive lactogenic immunity, especially mucosal IgA in milk, is critical for neonatal piglet protection
- sow vaccination or exposure claims must be handled carefully; unsafe feedback practices and fixed vaccine programs should not be generated casually
- no antiviral treatment should be presented; supportive management and diagnosis/biosecurity boundaries are the train-ready direction

Dataset impact: piglet diarrhea answers should distinguish TGE, PED, PDCoV, rotavirus, E. coli, coccidiosis, and management/passive-immunity context rather than using one generic "viral diarrhea" label.

### 11. Filovirus, flavivirus, HEV, herpesvirus, and influenza sections improve public-health and neurologic/reproductive boundaries

These sections add several disease-specific public-health and differential anchors:

- Reston ebolavirus in pigs has public-health/biosafety relevance distinct from typical swine production diseases; Ebola virus discussions require strict safety boundaries
- Japanese encephalitis virus is a reproductive and neurologic flavivirus with public-health and vector context; it belongs in abortion, stillbirth, weak-piglet, boar infertility, and neurologic differential matrices in endemic regions
- West Nile and Murray Valley encephalitis are mainly public-health/vector-context anchors rather than routine swine production diagnoses
- hepatitis E virus has zoonotic/food-safety relevance, often requiring public-health boundary handling rather than routine pig treatment
- PRV/Aujeszky disease affects piglets, older pigs, pregnant animals, boars, and non-porcine mammals differently; age, neurologic signs, respiratory signs, abortion, latency, wild/feral swine, semen, and DIVA/marker vaccine context are key
- PRV diagnosis requires laboratory confirmation; preferred tissues include trigeminal ganglia, olfactory ganglia, tonsils, and, depending on presentation, lung, spleen, liver, kidney, lymph nodes, pharyngeal mucosa, or fetal tissues
- influenza A in swine is a respiratory disease and a bidirectional human-swine interface; human-to-swine transmission is important, reassortment is common, and early influenza content should be treated as a public-health and respiratory differential anchor

Dataset impact: answers involving neurologic disease, abortions, respiratory outbreaks, show pigs, or human respiratory illness around pigs should retrieve this digest and relevant disease-specific pages.
